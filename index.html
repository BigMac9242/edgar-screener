The DuPont Plane

Return on capital employed isn't one number, it's two multiplied together. A company earns it either by charging a lot per unit of sales, or by pushing a lot of sales through a small capital base — and the two look identical in a ranking table. Plotted on log axes, every company with the same ROCE lands on the same diagonal. Where it sits along that line is the whole story.

ROCE  =  operating margin  ×  capital turnover  =  (EBIT ÷ revenue)  ×  (revenue ÷ capital employed)

What a high ROCE here does not mean

Domino's Pizza tops the unfiltered ranking at roughly 706%. That is arithmetic, not brilliance. Years of debt-funded buybacks left it with total assets of $1.74bn against current liabilities of $1.61bn, so capital employed collapses to about $125m and the denominator stops meaning anything. Equity is minus $3.96bn.

Any ratio with a small, unstable denominator will hand you a spectacular number. That's why rows whose capital employed is under a tenth of revenue carry a flag rather than a trophy.

The data is dirtier than it looks

Every figure here is lifted straight from XBRL tags in real filings, and filers tag things wrong. Some report in thousands where the unit says dollars. Some file twice under a parent and a subsidiary, so Charter and CCO Holdings appear as separate companies with identical numbers.

Fiscal years don't line up either — CY2024 holds NVIDIA's year ending January 2025 alongside Apple's ending September 2024. Comparing them is comparing different economies.

Reading the plane

Move up and you're a company charging a premium: software, pharma, payment networks. Move right and you're a company sweating a small asset base: distributors, grocers, staffing firms.

Visa and Costco earn respectable returns from opposite corners. Neither strategy is better — but only one of them survives a recession by cutting price, and the ranking table can't tell you which.
